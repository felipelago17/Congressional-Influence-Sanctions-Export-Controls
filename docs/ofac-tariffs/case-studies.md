# Case studies — tariffs as a sanctions tool, 2025–2026

Four episodes, in chronological order, that together show the executive testing IEEPA as a tariff authority, the courts closing that route, and Congress reopening it by statute — with a waiver. Each case lists its Tier 1 sources and the linkage grade the repository assigns.

---

## 1. EO 14245 — "secondary tariffs" on importers of Venezuelan oil (March 2025)

**What happened.** On 24 March 2025 the President signed Executive Order 14245, *Imposing Tariffs on Countries Importing Venezuelan Oil*, authorising the Secretary of State, on a Commerce determination, to impose a 25 percent tariff on all goods from any country importing Venezuelan oil directly or indirectly, effective 2 April 2025. The order invoked IEEPA and the existing Venezuela national emergency (EO 13692 and successors), and cited the Maduro government and Tren de Aragua (designated FTO/SDGT) as the threat.

**Why it matters.** This was the first use of the term "secondary tariff" in US practice — a tariff on a *third country* for trading with a sanctioned target, modelled on secondary sanctions but operating through customs duties rather than market-access denial. No country was ever designated under it.

**Outcome.** Rendered unlawful as a tariff measure by *Learning Resources* (February 2026). Practitioners treat the framework as void; the underlying Venezuela sanctions programme is unaffected.

**Sources (Tier 1).** [EO 14245, 90 FR 13829 (27 Mar 2025)](https://www.govinfo.gov/link/cpd/executiveorder/14245); [White House text](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/eo-14245.pdf).

**Linkage.** None — no congressional signal preceded it. Recorded as an executive action only.

## 2. The India secondary tariff — IEEPA used against a buyer of Russian oil (August 2025 – February 2026)

**What happened.** On 6 August 2025 an executive order under IEEPA imposed an additional 25 percent tariff on most imports from India, effective 27 August, on the ground that India was directly or indirectly importing Russian oil — stacking on the 25 percent "reciprocal" tariff already in place for a cumulative 50 percent. India objected that it was being penalised for doing what the G7 price-cap design invited. The duty was withdrawn on 6 February 2026 after India committed to stop the purchases.

**Why it matters.** The only secondary tariff actually *imposed* under IEEPA. It demonstrated the tool's coercive reach (a 50 percent wall on a major trading partner) and its fragility: two weeks after withdrawal the Supreme Court held the statutory basis never existed.

**Outcome.** Withdrawn by negotiation before the ruling; the ruling confirms it was invalid as imposed. Refund exposure now sits with the Court of International Trade.

**Sources.** Executive order of 6 August 2025 (Federal Register citation to be added); [Cassidy Levy Kent summary](https://www.cassidylevy.com/news/secondary-tariffs-to-be-imposed-on-imports-from-india/) and [Legal 500 note](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/india/international-law/trump-imposes-secondary-tariffs-on-india-targeting-russian-oil-imports) (Tier 3).

**Linkage.** `proximity-only` to the then-pending Graham bill (S. 1241 / H.R. 2548 in its 2025 form, which proposed 500 percent secondary tariffs). The executive acted while the bill was stalled; no documentary evidence that the bill caused the order. No inference drawn.

## 3. *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* — the Court removes the IEEPA route (February 2026)

**What happened.** On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court held 6–3 that IEEPA does not authorise the President to impose tariffs. The Chief Justice's opinion (joined in full by Gorsuch and Barrett, and in judgment by Kagan, Sotomayor and Jackson, who reached the same result on ordinary statutory construction without the major-questions doctrine) reasoned that tariffs are a tax reserved to Congress under Article I §8 and that "regulate … importation" is not a clear grant of that power. The D.D.C. case was dismissed for want of jurisdiction; the Court of International Trade's judgment in *V.O.S. Selections* was affirmed. The dissent (Kavanaugh, joined by Thomas and Alito) argued the text clearly covers tariffs and that the major-questions doctrine does not apply in foreign affairs — and noted that the majority's logic would presumably also bar IEEPA quotas.

**Why it matters for sanctions.** The holding is about tariffs only. Blocking, licensing and trade prohibitions under IEEPA are untouched — the Court expressly distinguished the sanctions and embargo powers Presidents have "most frequently" used. But the clear-statement reasoning now sits on the statute that underpins most OFAC programmes, and the dissent's quota point shows where the next challenge will be aimed. The administration ended IEEPA tariff collection effective 24 February 2026 but continued to use IEEPA to suspend the *de minimis* customs exemption — a use the ruling did not squarely address.

**Sources (Tier 1).** [Opinion, No. 24-1287 (Justia)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/607/24-1287/); [Cornell LII syllabus and opinions](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/24-1287); CRS Legal Sidebar LSB11398 (congress.gov; blocks automated retrieval — read directly).

**Linkage.** Not a congressional signal. Recorded under [judicial bounds](../influence/administration-levers.md#judicial-bounds-on-delegated-authority).

## 4. The Graham Act and its waiver — Congress legislates the tariff, the President waives it (July – October 2026)

**What happened.**
- The Senate advanced the *Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026* on 29 July 2026 (reported as 86–12 on the procedural vote and 86–11 on passage — the discrepancy is between advance and final votes and should be resolved against the Congress.gov roll call); the House passed H.R. 5334 262–159; signed 18 September 2026 as **Public Law 119-111**.
- The Act (i) directs tariffs of up to 500 percent on Russian-origin goods (§112); (ii) directs secondary tariffs of up to 100 percent on the top five importers of Russian crude or gas and the top five facilitators of Russian oil sanctions evasion, with USTR to redetermine the lists every 180 days (§113); (iii) codifies the existing EO-based Russia sanctions (§102(d)) and mandates further measures on banks, sovereign debt, energy, financial messaging, uranium and shadow-fleet vessels (§§102–111), most on a 30-day clock; (iv) permits presidential waiver of any sanction, restriction or duty on written certification to Congress that it is in the national interest, with a report (§115); and (v) allows termination only on certification of a peace agreement accepted by Ukraine and a cessation of hostilities, subject to congressional review (§117).
- The Act was drafted to survive *Learning Resources*: its tariffs rest on Article I authority delegated by statute, not on IEEPA. The 500 percent figure in the 2025 bill was cut to a 100 percent ceiling for the secondary tier; no minimum rate is specified.
- On 16 October 2026 an executive order, *Implementing the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026*, set the tariff rates (§2: +500 percent Russia; +100 percent each top-5 tier, triggered by new purchases on or after 18 October 2026) and then, in §4, **waived** all of them and all §§102–111 sanctions under §115, stating that the certifications and reports had been submitted. The waivers are open-ended — no expiry, no review period. This followed a 9 October deal permitting Russian diesel imports until April 2027, implemented through OFAC General License 135. Existing EO-based Russia sanctions and OFAC designations remain in force.

**Why it matters.** This is the repository's model case. A bipartisan congressional signal became a statutory mandate; the executive complied with the letter (rates set) and immediately exercised the waiver Congress wrote. Congress's remaining levers are exactly those the post-midterm commentary identifies: oversight of the certifications, hearings, and amending §115 — which would need a veto-proof majority. Practitioners describe the Act's measures as "dormant rather than defunct."

**Sources.** Tier 1: [H.R. 5334, Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5334) (Public Law 119-111; on the [watchlist](../trackers/congress-signals.md)); EO of 16 October 2026 (Federal Register citation to be added); OFAC GL 135 (OFAC URL to be added). Tier 3: [Sullivan & Cromwell](https://www.sullcrom.com/insights/memo/2026/September/Graham-Act-Expands-Russia-Tariffs-Sanctions), [Squire Patton Boggs](https://www.squirepattonboggs.com/insights/publications/sanctions-by-statute-the-graham-act-and-tariffs-on-russia-s-energy-buyers/), [Freshfields](https://www.freshfields.com/en/our-thinking/blogs/a-fresh-take/the-graham-act-bipartisan-sanctions-and-tariffs-waived-102o735), [Baker Botts](https://www.bakerbotts.com/thought-leadership/publications/2026/september/president-trump-signs-graham-sanctions-act-expanding-russia-sanctions-and-tariff-authorities), [Snell & Wilmer](https://www.swlaw.com/publication/executive-order-implements-and-immediately-waives-new-graham-act-sanctions-and-tariffs-targeting-russia/).

**Linkage.** `statutory-mandate`: Pub. L. 119-111 → EO of 16 October 2026. Recorded in `linkages.yaml`. The waiver is itself an exercise of statutory authority (§115), so the grade holds for both the implementation and the waiver.

## Reading across the four cases

| | Authority claimed | Congressional role | Court role | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EO 14245 (Venezuela) | IEEPA | None | Invalidated (2026) | Never applied |
| India tariff | IEEPA | Pending bill, no causal link | Invalidated (2026) | Withdrawn by deal |
| *Learning Resources* | — | — | Holding: IEEPA ≠ tariffs | Baseline reset |
| Graham Act + waiver | Statute (Art. I delegation) with §115 waiver | Mandate by supermajority | Not yet | Enacted, set, waived |

The sequence is the repository's thesis in miniature: the executive's unilateral instrument was removed by the Court, Congress supplied a statutory one, and the executive used the discretion Congress left in it. What a new Congress can do next is bounded by the same text — §115 and §117 — unless it can muster two-thirds.
