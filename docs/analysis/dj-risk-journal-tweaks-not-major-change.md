## Headline argument

A round-up of practitioner and think-tank views the day after the midterms. The consensus: the Democratic win changes the *politics* of sanctions, export controls and tariffs more than the *policy*. The President's unilateral authorities (IEEPA, tariffs, OFAC licensing, waivers) remain intact; Congress's new leverage lies in delay, oversight, confirmations and must-pass vehicles such as the NDAA.

This is the same conclusion as the [Sanctions Drop #18 note](sanctions-drop-18-democrats-take-congress.md) — unsurprisingly, since the same sanctions lawyer (Nick Turner, Akrivis Law) is quoted in both — but the Dow Jones piece widens the lens to Russia/Ukraine, Iran, China trade and Latin America.

## Views by theme

### Russia / Ukraine
- Maria Shagina (IISS) expects Congress to be able to *slow* implementation of some administration policies but not to reverse course on Russia or tariffs, partly because rolling back tariffs or legislating new Russia sanctions takes too long within a two-year window.
- She expects the administration to press harder, not retreat, across tariffs, Iran and Russia, and flags the risk that Moscow reads a weakened President as an opening to push for more sanctions relief and US business deals.

*Repository relevance:* reinforces the Graham Act / GL 135 thread already opened — the live question is whether congressional oversight can constrain further waivers, not whether new sanctions pass.

### Congress's actual levers
- Nick Turner (Akrivis Law): no "free hand" for Democrats. Filibuster and veto-override arithmetic still bind; the gains are in oversight, scrutiny of executive actions, nominee confirmations, and NDAA leverage.

*Repository relevance:* maps directly onto the **oversight**, **nominee holds** and **statutory mandates (NDAA)** levers in [Administration Levers](../influence/administration-levers.md).

### China and export controls
- Ting Peng (BZW, Shenzhen): US–China businesses now treat export controls and national-security screening as bipartisan fixtures; no expectation of relief from a change in Congress. She characterises Beijing's official line as non-involvement in US domestic politics and a preference for predictability in economic relations.
- Dawson Law (Conseil Global Advisors): the China trade truce is the deal the administration most wants to preserve (markets, farm exports), and Beijing will use that to seek concessions on export controls and investment restrictions. Congress on both sides is more hawkish and could lock restrictions into must-pass legislation that cannot be quickly undone. Any relaxation of China controls should be treated by business as provisional.

*Repository relevance:* two trackable hypotheses — (a) NDAA provisions hardening China controls (candidate `statutory-mandate` linkages, see the 1260H extension already noted), and (b) executive-side relaxations under the truce (e.g. the BIS Affiliates Rule suspension in `regulations-under-discussion.yaml`) that Congress may try to constrain.

### Iran
- Dawson Law: Tehran may read the result as time being on its side and keep to its existing playbook.

### Latin America / cartels
- Chris Urben (former DEA): expect the administration to double down on Venezuela, Cuba and anti-cartel policy. Even a future Democratic administration would likely keep cartel FTO designations — shifting from military tools toward DOJ arrests and extraditions — so business risk from FTO-related sanctions and material-support exposure stays flat or rises. DOJ evidence-gathering over the next two years would carry over regardless of who holds the presidency.

*Repository relevance:* FTO designations of cartels are OFAC/State actions with direct sanctions-compliance consequences; worth adding to the enforcement/regulatory watch as a standing theme rather than an election-dependent one.
