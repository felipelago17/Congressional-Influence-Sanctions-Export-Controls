## What is reported

- The Supreme People's Court published on **24 June 2026** a Shanghai Maritime Court judgment, among six representative 2025 maritime cases, that treats the **Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law (Art. 12)** as an overriding mandatory rule. A Singapore carrier that refused to issue a bill of lading and returned cargo out of fear of US sanctions exposure on a Hong Kong shipper was held in breach, and ordered to pay the cargo value (about RMB 5 million) plus interest. There was no appeal.
- Per Norton Rose Fulbright, the carrier cited the Chinese consignor's **BIS Entity List** status and high-risk destinations; the court held a governing-law clause cannot exclude the AFSL, found a fundamental breach, and said foreign unilateral sanctions cannot serve as a defence to non-performance. Neither contracting party was based in mainland China, and neither appealed.
- Commentators read it as the **first judicial application of the AFSL**, and say foreign sanctions concerns, and possibly sanctions clauses, may not excuse non-performance before Chinese courts.

## Why it is recorded in this repository

The repository tracks how elections and Congress influence executive action on sanctions and export controls. Sanctions designations that Congress presses for (and OFAC and BIS administer) now meet a **countermeasure that works through courts**: foreign firms that comply with a US listing can be held liable in China. That affects how much leverage US listings carry for companies with Chinese exposure.
