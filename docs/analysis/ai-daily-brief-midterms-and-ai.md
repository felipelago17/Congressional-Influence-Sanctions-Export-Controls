# Related — *The AI Daily Brief*, "How the Midterm Results Change AI"

**Source:** NLW, *The AI Daily Brief: Artificial Intelligence News and Analysis*, episode of 4 November 2026 (~25 min) — [Spotify](https://open.spotify.com/episode/0jJBuNIQB3dFmVsVPaenkG)

**Source tier:** Tier 3 (podcast commentary). **Basis of this note:** two partial transcripts — the opening ~7 minutes (polling, campaign framing, CA-11, start of Ohio) and a later ~7-minute segment (policy outlook, export controls, data-centre politics, the downside scenario). The Ohio detail and the 2028 discussion are not covered. Paraphrased in the repository's own words; figures are as the host relays them and are attributed to his sources, not verified against them.

**Scope:** adjacent to this repository. AI regulation and data-centre politics are not sanctions or export controls, but the host addresses export controls directly (§3 below), and the chip-control and remote-access threads (RASA, advanced-computing rules) run through the same committees.

## 1. The result, and what was on the ballot (opening segment)

**Result as framed.** A Democratic wave with an ~11-point national swing, control of both chambers, no veto-proof supermajority; smaller in seat terms than 2010 or 2018 but with flips in Texas, South Carolina and Kentucky. The host's correction to the "AI was on the ballot" framing: *data centres* were on the ballot, not AI as such.

**Polling the host cites.**
- Pew (early October): 44% of voters called AI a very important issue — below foreign policy (57%), immigration (59%), education (62%), health care (68%), democracy (73%) and the economy (84%). The issue skewed Democratic (54% vs 34%).
- Pew on data centres: 61% dislike or strongly dislike candidates who support local data-centre construction (75% of Democratic supporters, 44% of Republican); only 5% and 14% respectively *like* such candidates.
- NBC exit poll: 56% called data centres a crucial or major factor (Democrats twice as likely as Republicans), against immigration enforcement 73%, the Iran war 74%, cost of living 89%, and government corruption 90% — last among the hot-button issues but high enough to be polled.

**How it was campaigned.** Data-centre opposition was folded into economy and "oligarchy" themes: Sherrod Brown (Ohio) on Ohioans not subsidising costs and deciding locally; Abdul El-Sayed (Michigan) tying Flock cameras and data centres to Mike Rogers' Patriot Act vote. Republicans were boxed in by the President's late-August line that communities rejecting data centres would end up "backwards and poor"; few split from him.

**Races where AI was central.** *CA-11 (San Francisco)* — Scott Wiener (SB 1047 sponsor) leading Connie Chan at recording time; Wiener campaigned on having fought big tech over AI regulation, Chan attacked his funding from an Anthropic-aligned pro-regulation PAC; the host reads Wiener as a future champion for existential-risk-focused safety advocates. *Ohio* — ~200 existing data centres, 46 planned at a combined 16 GW, led by Meta's Prometheus site outside Columbus (transcript cuts off).

## 2. Ballot measures and the "narrative war" (later segment)

The host reports **41 down-ballot measures** on data centres across the country, all 41 appearing to pass as of election night, and quotes Brad Carson (Americans for Responsible Innovation) calling it an "AI earthquake." His own gloss: AI companies have lost the narrative war on the build-out.

## 3. Federal policy outlook — including export controls

**Ceiling.** No comprehensive federal AI framework and no federal moratorium on data centres or frontier training, because the President has said he will veto freely ("Mr. Veto," per a pre-election Fox interview) and Democrats lack an override majority.

**Floor — what the host expects can pass, because it is bipartisan "low-hanging fruit":**
- whistleblower protections for AI-lab employees;
- codifying the executive branch's voluntary pre-release testing of frontier models;
- incident-reporting rules;
- stronger statutory liability for model builders;
- possibly data-sovereignty rules.

**Export controls — his explicit caveat.** He singles out export controls as the area where the outcome is *hardest to read*: China policy and export-control design have not split along party lines, so there is "no easy extrapolation" from a Democratic Congress to more or fewer controls. He expects it to be an area of debate, tied to discussion of pausing, pacing, or prohibiting certain training techniques (recursive self-improvement). Hearings with AI executives will be frequent but, in his view, not particularly impactful.

**Underlying driver.** He argues the shift toward more rules was coming regardless of the result, because more capable models produce a "natural ramp-up" in demanded controls; the election accelerates a trend rather than creating it. His optimism rests on how many foundational issues have cross-party consensus.

## 4. Data centres — the new settlement

The election confirms, rather than creates, the end of the era in which developers did not need to court communities. Changes he says are already under way: moratoria on NDAs, developers paying their own way on infrastructure and electricity, and community incentives. He distinguishes irreconcilable opponents from new leaders who understand the economic stakes and will write "hard but achievable" rules — citing Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa's line that she does not hate data centres but what they do to ordinary Texans. His view: the most "winnable" political issue he has seen, for all sides.

**Downside scenario he sketches.** Moratoria gain momentum → executive action by new governors → Wall Street withdraws funding → build-out stalls → neocloud failures → broader downturn, because the AI build-out has been the main growth story — leaving 2028 debated through the lens of a burst bubble as well as jobs, cyber and existential risk.

## Why it is filed here

- **Export controls flagged as non-partisan.** The host's point that China/export-control positions do not map onto party lines is consistent with the repository's own finding on the Remote Access Security Act (cosponsors D 9 / R 8 in the House) and with the Graham Act majorities. It argues against reading the midterm result as a directional signal on BIS policy — the same conclusion the specialist sources reached ([cross-source check](midterms-2026-cross-source-check.md)).
- **Veto ceiling, bipartisan floor.** His structure — nothing comprehensive, but a list of narrow bipartisan measures — is the AI-policy version of the oversight-not-legislation consensus in the other notes.
- **Data-centre siting and chip controls intersect.** Where compute is built, and under what conditions, is the domestic face of the question export controls address abroad.
- **Member references are context, not scorecards.** Wiener, Brown, El-Sayed and Hinojosa are noted because the host made them central; the repository aggregates by party and committee only.
- **Keyword scan coverage.** `artificial intelligence` and `semiconductor` are already in the Congress.gov watchlist keywords; the bipartisan items he lists (whistleblower, incident reporting, liability) are candidates for new keywords if they surface as bills.
